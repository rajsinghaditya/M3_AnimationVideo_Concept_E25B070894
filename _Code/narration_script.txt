SCENE 1 — Hook (~30 seconds)
------------------------------
Imagine you have three equations with three unknowns — x, y, and z.
You could try plugging in numbers at random, or substituting one equation into another.
But with three equations, that quickly becomes messy and error-prone.
What if there were a systematic method — an algorithm — that always works, no matter how complicated the system gets?
There is. It's called Gaussian elimination, and that's exactly what we'll explore today.


SCENE 2 — Concept Introduction (~50 seconds)
----------------------------------------------
Let's start with the basics.
A system of linear equations can be written in matrix form as A x equals b,
where A is the coefficient matrix, x is the vector of unknowns, and b is the vector of constants.
We combine A and b into the augmented matrix, written with a vertical bar separating them.

To solve the system, we use three elementary row operations.
First, swapping two rows.
Second, multiplying a row by a nonzero scalar.
Third, adding a multiple of one row to another row — also called a row replacement.

These operations transform the augmented matrix into row echelon form,
where each leading entry — called a pivot — is to the right of the pivot above it,
and all entries below each pivot are zero.

Here is our learning objective:
By the end of this video, the viewer will be able to explain how Gaussian elimination uses row operations to reduce a 3-by-3 system to row echelon form, why those operations do not change the solution set, and what the solution means geometrically.


SCENE 3 — Visual Intuition (~50 seconds)
------------------------------------------
Now let's build some geometric intuition.
Each linear equation in three variables represents a plane in three-dimensional space.
Here we see three coloured planes — blue, green, and orange — corresponding to our three equations.
The solution to the system is the single point where all three planes intersect.

Notice that point. It's at x equals 1, y equals 2, z equals 3.

When we perform a row operation — say, replacing Row 2 with Row 2 minus two times Row 1 — we're replacing one plane with a different plane.
But here's the key insight: the new plane still passes through the same intersection point.
So the solution set never changes. That's why row operations are safe — they preserve the solution.


SCENE 4 — Worked Example (~100 seconds)
-----------------------------------------
Let's work through the full example. Our system is:
x plus y plus z equals 6,
2x plus 3y plus z equals 11,
x plus 2y plus 3z equals 14.

First, we write the augmented matrix.
Row 1 is 1, 1, 1, bar, 6.
Row 2 is 2, 3, 1, bar, 11.
Row 3 is 1, 2, 3, bar, 14.

The first pivot is the 1 in position Row 1, Column 1.

Step one: eliminate the entry below the pivot in Row 2.
We compute R2 minus 2 times R1.
2 minus 2 is 0. 3 minus 2 is 1. 1 minus 2 is negative 1. 11 minus 12 is negative 1.
So the new Row 2 is 0, 1, negative 1, bar, negative 1.

Step two: eliminate the entry below the pivot in Row 3.
We compute R3 minus R1.
1 minus 1 is 0. 2 minus 1 is 1. 3 minus 1 is 2. 14 minus 6 is 8.
So the new Row 3 is 0, 1, 2, bar, 8.

Step three: eliminate the entry below the second pivot in Row 3.
We compute R3 minus R2.
0 minus 0 is 0. 1 minus 1 is 0. 2 minus negative 1 is 3. 8 minus negative 1 is 9.
So the new Row 3 is 0, 0, 3, bar, 9.

The matrix is now in row echelon form, with pivots at positions 1-1, 2-2, and 3-3.

Now we back-substitute.
From Row 3: 3z equals 9, so z equals 3.
From Row 2: y minus z equals negative 1, so y equals negative 1 plus 3, which is 2.
From Row 1: x plus y plus z equals 6, so x equals 6 minus 2 minus 3, which is 1.

The solution is x equals 1, y equals 2, z equals 3.


SCENE 5 — Interpretation (~40 seconds)
----------------------------------------
Let's verify our solution by substituting back into the original equations.
Equation 1: 1 plus 2 plus 3 equals 6. Check!
Equation 2: 2 times 1 plus 3 times 2 plus 3 equals 2 plus 6 plus 3 equals 11. Check!
Equation 3: 1 plus 2 times 2 plus 3 times 3 equals 1 plus 4 plus 9 equals 14. Check!

This confirms that the point (1, 2, 3) lies on all three planes — it is the unique intersection point.

But not every system has a unique solution.
If elimination produces a row like 0, 0, 0, bar, 5 — that's a contradiction. The system has no solution, and the planes don't all meet at a common point.
If instead we get 0, 0, 0, bar, 0 — there may be infinitely many solutions, and the planes intersect along a line or overlap entirely.
